Incentive Compatibility in Experiments with Incomplete Preference

Draft available upon request

Abstract

Experiments that elicit preferences almost always presume completeness, one of the most contested axioms. We ask how much incentive compatibility survives when preferences may be incomplete, maintaining transitivity. To tell genuine incomparability apart from indifference, we enrich the outcome space with a sure monetary dimension under a mild richness condition on the incomparable set. Full strict incentive compatibility is unattainable under incompleteness. The exact frontier is comparable-side strict incentive compatibility: subjects always weakly prefer truthful reports, and strictly so whenever two alternatives are comparable. This yields a conservative identification that never overstates incomparability. We construct a domain-free mechanism attaining this frontier.

Incomparability and Status Quo Bias

with Yoram Halevy

Draft available upon request

Abstract

Status quo bias is one of the leading robust behaviors that challenge standard economic modeling, but its source has been debated in theoretical and experimental studies. We provide experimental evidence that accounting for incomplete preferences is crucial in diagnosing SQB. We develop an identification strategy that bounds the behavior of a very general class of complete preferences and uses violations of these bounds as indication of incomparability. Implementing this strategy in a laboratory experiment, we find that incomplete preferences are widespread, with roughly one third of subjects exhibiting incomparability. These subjects are significantly more likely to retain the status quo when facing incomparable alternatives, and display substantially higher status quo bias than subjects whose choices are consistent with complete preferences. Furthermore, we demonstrate that ignoring preference incompleteness is empirically consequential: once subjects with incomplete preferences are excluded, the evidence against the Coase Theorem is no longer robust, and the case for the Endowment Effect largely disappears.
